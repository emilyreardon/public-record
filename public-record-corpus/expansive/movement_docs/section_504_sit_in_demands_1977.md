================================================================================
BACKGROUND: SECTION 504 AND FOUR YEARS OF DELAY
================================================================================

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 was one sentence: "No otherwise qualified handicapped individual in the United States shall, solely by reason of his handicap, be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance."

It was the first federal civil rights law to prohibit discrimination against people with disabilities. Congress passed it; President Nixon signed it. Then nothing happened.

For four years — through the Nixon, Ford, and into the Carter administrations — the Department of Health, Education and Welfare (HEW) refused to issue the implementing regulations that would give Section 504 the force of law. Without regulations, the law was unenforceable. HEW secretaries stalled, rewrote drafts to weaken the protections, and failed to sign.

Disability rights organizers understood what the delay meant: the law on paper meant nothing without the regulations. They organized.

================================================================================
THE DEMANDS
================================================================================

On April 5, 1977, protesters coordinated sit-ins at HEW offices in ten cities across the country, demanding that newly confirmed HEW Secretary Joseph Califano sign the Section 504 regulations without modification. Protesters in New York, Chicago, Los Angeles, Boston, Denver, Atlanta, Philadelphia, Seattle, and Washington D.C. occupied regional HEW offices.

The core demand was simple and non-negotiable: sign the regulations as written — unchanged from the version that had been drafted — within a fixed deadline.

Demonstrators specifically opposed proposed weakening amendments that would have:
- Narrowed the definition of "handicapped individual" to exclude people with certain conditions
- Allowed federally funded programs to continue excluding disabled people if "reasonable accommodation" was deemed too costly
- Delayed the accessibility requirements for buildings and transportation

The American Coalition of Citizens with Disabilities (ACCD), the lead organizing body, rejected any revision to the substantive protections. Protesters argued that further delay and revision were themselves forms of discrimination — the perpetuation of a system that treated disabled people as burdens to be accommodated only when convenient.

================================================================================
THE SAN FRANCISCO OCCUPATION, APRIL 5–30, 1977
================================================================================

In most cities, the occupations lasted a few hours. In San Francisco, more than 100 protesters — wheelchair users, blind people, Deaf people, people with a range of physical and psychiatric disabilities — occupied the HEW Regional Office in the Federal Building at 50 United Nations Plaza and refused to leave.

They stayed for 26 days. It became the longest occupation of a U.S. federal building in American history.

Conditions inside were difficult from the start. Federal officials cut off phone lines. They refused to allow food deliveries. They threatened demonstrators with removal.

The protesters held. The Black Panther Party delivered hot meals every night. The Butterfly Brigade provided medical care. Machinists' Union members brought sleeping bags and supplies. Deaf community members facilitated communication. The City of San Francisco provided portable toilets. Mayor George Moscone refused to allow police to remove the protesters.

Inside the building, occupiers organized. They held daily meetings, assigned committees, maintained a communications team, managed medical needs, and sustained 24-hour occupation of the federal offices for nearly a month.

Brad Lomax, a Black Panther member who used a wheelchair, was one of the key organizers coordinating with the Black Panther Party's support network. Judith Heumann, a co-founder of Disabled in Action and later the Center for Independent Living, was a central leader of the occupation and the negotiations.

================================================================================
CALIFANO SIGNS, APRIL 28, 1977
================================================================================

A delegation of occupiers traveled to Washington, D.C. to meet with Califano directly. He declined to meet. They met with Vice President Mondale instead. The occupation continued.

On April 28, 1977 — after 26 days — Secretary Califano signed the Section 504 implementing regulations without the weakening amendments protesters had opposed. The regulations took effect.

The protesters left the building April 30, 1977.

================================================================================
SIGNIFICANCE
================================================================================

The Section 504 sit-in is the founding moment of the modern American disability rights movement. It demonstrated that disabled people could organize politically across disability type, race, age, and geography; that civil disobedience could be effective even when the participants used wheelchairs and ventilators; and that federal civil rights law required organized enforcement pressure, not just passage.

The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) of 1990 was built directly on Section 504's legal framework. Judith Heumann, Marca Bristo, Justin Dart Jr., and many 504 veterans were central to the ADA's passage. The Section 504 regulations signed by Califano in 1977 became the model for the ADA's implementing regulations thirteen years later.

SECTION 504 TEXT (FOR REFERENCE)
================================================================================

Rehabilitation Act of 1973, Public Law 93-112, Section 504:

"No otherwise qualified handicapped individual in the United States, as defined in section 7(6), shall, solely by reason of his handicap, be excluded from the participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance."
