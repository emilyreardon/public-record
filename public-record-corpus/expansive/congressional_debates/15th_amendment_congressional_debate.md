================================================================================
FIFTEENTH AMENDMENT (Final Adopted Text)
Passed by Congress February 26, 1869. Ratified February 3, 1870.
================================================================================

Section 1. The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude.

Section 2. The Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation.

================================================================================
CONTEXT: THE FIGHT OVER THE AMENDMENT'S LANGUAGE
================================================================================

The 15th Amendment was not the simple, inevitable measure it might appear in retrospect. Congress debated three substantially different versions in January–February 1869, with Radical and Moderate Republicans sharply divided over how sweeping the protections should be. The final text — the most narrow of the proposals — was the product of a legislative compromise that left significant vulnerabilities, as decades of post-Reconstruction disenfranchisement would prove.

African American men had voted in large numbers across the South in the 1868 presidential election, and referenda in Iowa and Minnesota had enfranchised Black men in those states that autumn. But Black men remained disfranchised in 11 of the 21 Northern and Western states and the five Border States — a combined area where one-sixth of American Black people lived. Northern Black communities petitioned Congress for a constitutional amendment.

In January 1869, the National Convention of Colored Men of America met in Washington, D.C., advocating suffrage for all Black men and the education of former slaves. A committee of twelve called on President-elect Ulysses S. Grant, urging vigilance in the fulfillment and administration of equal rights. Grant pledged as president to uphold equal protection under the law.

Republicans of the outgoing 40th Congress faced overlapping pressures: protect Black voting rights in the South from repeal by future state legislatures, enfranchise Northern Black men, and maintain a viable Republican coalition across regions with conflicting interests on voter qualifications. Some Republicans from New England and the Far West favored state literacy and nativity qualifications for voters, aimed at Irish and Chinese immigrants. Radicals wanted a broad amendment barring disfranchisement on grounds of race, property, literacy, and other classifications. Moderates believed it should be limited to race-based disfranchisement, leaving other qualifications to the states.

================================================================================
THE THREE COMPETING PROPOSALS
================================================================================

THE BOUTWELL PROPOSAL (House, January 30, 1869)

Republican George Boutwell of Massachusetts introduced what would become essentially the final form of the Amendment: prohibit the federal and state governments from denying the vote to citizens based on "race, or color, or previous condition of slavery." The House passed this moderate version on January 30, 1869, by a vote of 150-42.

Harper's Weekly editor George William Curtis praised Boutwell's proposal as "another of those great measures which commend it to the confidence of all thoughtful men, and to the gratitude of posterity." He explained that it was better to protect equal suffrage through a constitutional amendment than through a federal law.

THE WILSON PROPOSAL (Senate, February 9, 1869)

The full Senate narrowly approved, 31-27, a proposed amendment introduced by Republican Henry Wilson of Massachusetts that went further: it prohibited the federal and state governments from discriminating in voting or office holding based on "race, color, nativity, property, education, or [religious] creed." This was the most expansive version — it would have prohibited literacy tests and property qualifications nationwide.

Harper's Weekly editor Curtis responded cautiously. He emphasized the educational aspect of Wilson's amendment and cautioned the House not to rush to judgment, expressing unconvinced that the amendment should ban all educational requirements in all circumstances.

THE STEWART PROPOSAL (Senate, February 17, 1869)

The Senate Judiciary Committee had introduced a separate amendment, drafted by Republican William Stewart of Nevada, similar to Boutwell's proposal but also banning denial of public office based on race, color, or previous status as a slave. On February 17, 1869, the full Senate passed Stewart's moderate version, 35-11 (with 20 senators absent).

THE BINGHAM PROPOSAL (House, February 19, 1869)

On February 15, the House defeated the Senate's Wilson bill. Four days later, it passed, 140-33, a version introduced by Republican John Bingham of Ohio — nearly identical to Wilson's proposal except that it applied only against the states, not the federal government.

================================================================================
CONFERENCE AND FINAL PASSAGE
================================================================================

The Joint Congressional Committee accepted Stewart's moderate Senate measure while dropping the reference to office holding, thus essentially returning to Boutwell's proposal originally passed by the House on January 30. In less than a month, Congress had come full circle: the House originally passed a moderate version and the Senate passed a radical one; the House rejected the Senate resolution and then approved its own radical amendment; the Senate then passed a moderate one; and the conference committee adopted a moderate version that was basically the first House-approved proposal.

Backed by large Republican majorities, the compromise resolution passed the House on February 25, 1869, by a vote of 143-43, and the Senate on February 26, 1869, by a vote of 39-13 (with 14 absent).

================================================================================
CONTEMPORARY EDITORIAL RESPONSE
================================================================================

Harper's Weekly (February 13, 1869):
On Boutwell's original House proposal: "another of those great measures which commend it to the confidence of all thoughtful men, and to the gratitude of posterity." The editorial explained that it was better to protect equal suffrage through a constitutional amendment than a federal law.

Harper's Weekly (February 27, 1869):
On Wilson's broader Senate proposal — which would have prohibited literacy, property, and nativity qualifications: The editor "emphasized the educational aspect of Wilson's amendment, and cautioned the House not to judge the measure rashly, but to consider seriously how best to ensure an educated electorate. The editor professed little faith in educational tests for voters, but was unconvinced that the amendment should ban all educational requirements in all circumstances."

================================================================================
SIGNIFICANCE OF THE NARROW FINAL TEXT
================================================================================

Although the final form of the Fifteenth Amendment was moderate compared to the other proposals, it presented a significant change in the U.S. Constitution. It prohibited the denial or abridgment of the vote on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude. However, by leaving literacy tests, poll taxes, grandfather clauses, and other mechanisms untouched, the narrow text created the conditions for systematic post-Reconstruction disenfranchisement. African Americans exercised the right to vote across the South through the 1880s; by the early 1890s, Southern states had enacted the devices that would suppress Black political participation for nearly a century. The promise of the 15th Amendment would require the Voting Rights Act of 1965 — 95 years later — to begin to be fully realized.
