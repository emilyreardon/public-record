================================================================================
NINETEENTH AMENDMENT (Final Adopted Text)
Passed by Congress June 4, 1919. Ratified August 18, 1920.
================================================================================

Section 1. The right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex.

Section 2. The Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation.

================================================================================
CONTEXT: THE ROAD TO PASSAGE
================================================================================

The 19th Amendment had been introduced in every Congress since 1878, when Senator Aaron Sargent of California first proposed identical language derived from the 15th Amendment. For forty years, it failed to advance.

The House first passed the amendment on January 10, 1918, by a vote of 274 to 136 — exactly the required two-thirds majority. The Senate defeated it twice that year. The amendment died in the 65th Congress.

When Republicans swept the 1918 midterm elections and took control of the House, the new Speaker, Frederick Gillette of Massachusetts, made passage of the suffrage amendment an early priority of the 66th Congress. The amendment came to the floor again on May 21, 1919.

================================================================================
HOUSE FLOOR DEBATE, MAY 21, 1919
================================================================================

Members in favor drew on decades of women's civic and wartime service as the basis for extending the franchise.

Representative John Raker (D-CA), a longtime suffrage supporter, argued the vote was overdue: "Because of the work women have done in our country and in this war, they should be given the same right to participate in their Government as the men have."

Representative John Little (R-KY) put the argument in starker terms: "For centuries you have treated women as a slave. She has been taxed without representation. She has been governed without her consent. Give her now a fixed, reasonable status."

Representative Knute Nelson (R-MN) invoked the taxation argument that had driven debates since the colonial era: "Taxation without representation should no longer apply to womankind."

The House passed the amendment on May 21, 1919, by a vote of 304 to 89 — well above the required two-thirds.

================================================================================
SENATE PASSAGE, JUNE 4, 1919
================================================================================

The Senate had been the principal obstacle to the amendment for decades. Southern Democrats opposed it most vocally, arguing that federal suffrage legislation violated states' rights and could be used to enforce Black women's voting in the South.

On June 4, 1919, the Senate passed the amendment 56 to 25, meeting the two-thirds threshold. The margin was narrow; opponents had sought further delay.

================================================================================
RATIFICATION, 1919–1920
================================================================================

Thirty-six states were required to ratify. The process came down to Tennessee, the 36th state, where the state legislature ratified on August 18, 1920 — with a one-vote margin in the Tennessee House. The decisive vote was cast by 24-year-old Harry T. Burn, the youngest member of the legislature, who changed his vote after receiving a letter from his mother urging him to "be a good boy" and vote for suffrage.

Secretary of State Bainbridge Colby certified ratification on August 26, 1920. An estimated 8 million women voted in the November 1920 presidential election.

================================================================================
LIMITATIONS AND CONTINUED EXCLUSIONS
================================================================================

The 19th Amendment formally prohibited denial of the vote on account of sex, but its practical effect was limited by intersecting disenfranchisement systems. Poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, and outright intimidation continued to prevent most Black women in the South from voting. Asian American women were ineligible for citizenship under the Chinese Exclusion Act and its successors. Native American women did not gain citizenship until 1924, and full voting access came even later in some states. The amendment's protection was meaningful primarily for white women in Northern and Western states.

The Voting Rights Act of 1965 was required to begin enforcing voting rights that the 15th and 19th Amendments had formally guaranteed for decades.
