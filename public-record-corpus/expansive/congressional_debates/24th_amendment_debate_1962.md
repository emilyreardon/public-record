================================================================================
TWENTY-FOURTH AMENDMENT (Final Adopted Text)
Passed by Congress August 27, 1962. Ratified January 23, 1964.
================================================================================

Section 1. The right of citizens of the United States to vote in any primary or other election for President or Vice President, for electors for President or Vice President, or for Senator or Representative in Congress, shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or any State by reason of failure to pay any poll tax or other tax.

Section 2. The Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation.

================================================================================
CONTEXT: POLL TAXES AND DISENFRANCHISEMENT
================================================================================

Poll taxes — fees required before a citizen could vote — had been used in Southern states since Reconstruction to prevent Black citizens from voting. By the 1960s, five states still maintained poll taxes for federal elections: Virginia, Alabama, Mississippi, Arkansas, and Texas.

Poll taxes operated as a compounding mechanism of exclusion. The fee itself was small — typically $1 to $2 per year — but in states that required payment of all back taxes before voting, a person who had never voted could face accumulated years of fees. In some states, the requirement had to be met months before an election, further reducing access. The taxes affected poor white voters as well, and were often paired with literacy tests, grandfather clauses, and other devices to selectively administer disenfranchisement.

Congress had debated abolishing the poll tax for federal elections since the 1940s. Multiple bills passed the House but were blocked in the Senate by Southern Democrats who controlled key committee chairmanships and used the filibuster to kill the legislation.

================================================================================
SENATE DEBATE AND PASSAGE, MARCH 1962
================================================================================

Senator Spessard Holland of Florida, himself a Southern Democrat, had championed the anti-poll tax amendment since 1949. Holland's strategy was to pursue a constitutional amendment rather than ordinary legislation, calculating that the amendment route would survive legal challenge and be harder for opponents to filibuster procedurally.

When the amendment came to the Senate floor in early 1962, opponents mounted a ten-day filibuster. Supporters invoked cloture — a procedure requiring two-thirds of senators present — and broke the filibuster.

The Senate passed the amendment on March 27, 1962, by a vote of 77 to 16.

Holland argued the amendment was about the integrity of democratic participation itself: requiring payment of a fee as a condition of voting had no legitimate constitutional basis. "The payment of a poll tax," he argued, "is in no way related to the qualifications for voting."

================================================================================
HOUSE DEBATE AND PASSAGE, AUGUST 1962
================================================================================

In the House, debate focused on the scope of the amendment. The final text prohibited poll taxes only in federal elections — primaries and general elections for President, Vice President, and Congress. Opponents of a broader ban argued that extending the prohibition to state and local elections would encroach on states' rights to set voter qualifications.

The House passed the amendment on August 27, 1962, by a vote of 295 to 86.

================================================================================
RATIFICATION, 1962–1964
================================================================================

The amendment was ratified in 16 months — ratification by 38 states was required. South Dakota became the 38th state to ratify on January 23, 1964, before the presidential election year. None of the five poll-tax states ratified; Mississippi formally ratified in 1989.

================================================================================
LIMITS OF THE AMENDMENT AND SUBSEQUENT HISTORY
================================================================================

The 24th Amendment applied only to federal elections. In Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections (1966), the Supreme Court extended the prohibition to state elections under the Equal Protection Clause of the 14th Amendment, effectively ending poll taxes in all elections.

Poll taxes were part of a system, not a standalone obstacle. The Voting Rights Act of 1965, passed the following year, directly addressed literacy tests, discriminatory administration, and other barriers that the 24th Amendment did not reach. Even after both the amendment and the Act, enforcement required sustained litigation and federal oversight.
