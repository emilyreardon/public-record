================================================================================
TWENTY-SIXTH AMENDMENT (Final Adopted Text)
Passed by Congress March 23, 1971. Ratified July 1, 1971.
================================================================================

Section 1. The right of citizens of the United States, who are eighteen years of age or older, to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of age.

Section 2. The Congress shall have power to enforce this article by appropriate legislation.

================================================================================
CONTEXT: THE QUESTION OF YOUNG PERSONS' KNOWLEDGE
================================================================================

The push to lower the voting age to 18 had existed since World War II, when the military draft applied to men as young as 18. The slogan "old enough to fight, old enough to vote" framed the argument for decades. Congress and most state legislatures declined to act.

The movement intensified during the Vietnam War. By 1971, 58,000 Americans had died in Vietnam; hundreds of thousands of draftees were under 21, the minimum voting age in most states. Young people protesting the war had no vote on the politicians who sent them to fight.

Congress tried to resolve this through ordinary legislation. In 1970, as part of the Voting Rights Act extension, Congress lowered the voting age to 18 for all elections. The Supreme Court in Oregon v. Mitchell (1970) upheld the change for federal elections but struck it down for state elections, ruling 5-4 that Congress lacked the power to set voting ages in state contests.

The ruling created an immediate administrative problem: states would have to maintain two separate voter rolls — one for voters 18-20 (eligible for federal races only) and one for voters 21 and older (eligible for all races). Running a dual-age election system was expensive and logistically chaotic. The practical argument united both parties behind a constitutional amendment.

================================================================================
SENATE PASSAGE, MARCH 10, 1971
================================================================================

The Senate moved quickly after Oregon v. Mitchell. On March 10, 1971, the Senate passed the 26th Amendment unanimously — every senator present voted in favor.

Senate floor debate centered on two themes: the injustice of taxing and drafting citizens who could not vote, and the argument that young people had demonstrated the civic knowledge and engagement that suffrage required. Senators on both sides cited the energy, information, and commitment young voters would bring to democratic participation.

================================================================================
HOUSE DEBATE AND PASSAGE, MARCH 23, 1971
================================================================================

The House took up the amendment on March 23, 1971. The debate was brief; the outcome was not in serious doubt given the practical and political consensus.

Representative Richard Poff (R-VA) put the case plainly during floor debate: "Today we act to correct that inequity. We extend to young persons who are 18, 19, and 20 years of age the right that is the heart of our democratic system — the right to vote."

Members argued that young Americans had proved themselves in the information age. They read newspapers, watched news broadcasts, organized politically, and had demonstrated on the streets that they understood what was at stake in national decisions — including decisions they had no legal power to influence.

The House passed the amendment on March 23, 1971, by a vote of 401 to 19.

================================================================================
RATIFICATION, 1971
================================================================================

The 26th Amendment was ratified in 83 days — the fastest ratification of any constitutional amendment in American history. Thirty-eight states ratified; the 38th, North Carolina, ratified on July 1, 1971. The amendment took effect in time for the 1972 presidential election.

An estimated 11 million new voters between the ages of 18 and 20 became eligible to vote. Their registration and turnout in 1972 was lower than other age groups — a pattern that has persisted, with significant variation, across subsequent elections.

================================================================================
COST OF THE DUAL-AGE SYSTEM
================================================================================

The administrative argument that clinched the amendment's passage is worth noting. States estimated that running parallel voter rolls — one for 18-to-20-year-olds in federal races only, one for all other voters — would cost millions of dollars and create significant ballot confusion. The constitutional amendment was, in part, chosen because it was cheaper and simpler than the alternative the Supreme Court had forced.

This dynamic — constitutional rights advanced partly on administrative efficiency grounds — is rarely discussed in histories of the amendment. The moral argument about representation was genuine; the logistics argument is what made the timeline possible.
